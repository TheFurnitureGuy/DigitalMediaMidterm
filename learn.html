How digital color works

How the photo editor reduces colors, and why PS2 games looked the way they did.

Why does it look different?

Your processed photo uses far fewer colors than the original. Here’s how that works.

RGB color

Every pixel on your screen mixes red, green and blue light. Each of the three has a brightness from 0 to 255, which gives about 16.7 million possible colors. For example, red 255, green 140 and blue 0 makes a bright orange.

Palettes and quantization

A palette is the short list of colors an image is allowed to use. Quantization builds that list from your photo, then changes each pixel to the closest color on it. So four slightly different blues in a sky can all become the same blue.

Dithering

When there aren’t enough colors, a smooth sky turns into stripes. This is called banding. Dithering hides it by mixing palette colors in a fine pattern of dots, which your eye blends into the shades in between. Many PS2 games used a small, repeating checkered pattern for this.

What does each setting change?

Output resolution and Palette size each reduce one part of the image. The look settings work differently, because they change the photo before its colors are reduced.

Output resolution

Fewer pixels

Resolution sets how many pixels the image has. With fewer pixels, you lose fine detail, but each pixel can still use any color in the palette. Softness also blurs detail, but it doesn’t change the number of pixels.

Palette size

Fewer colors

Palette size sets the most colors the image can use. The number of pixels stays the same. A simple photo can end up using fewer colors than the limit.

Look settings

Changes made first

Glow, fog, tint, grain and the TV effect change the photo before its colors are reduced. They add new shades, such as haze in the sky, so the palette ends up with different colors.

Why PS2 games looked like this

The presets copy effects that PS2 games used to set a mood or to hide what the console couldn’t do. Like the look settings, these effects change the colors before the palette is chosen.

Fog

In Silent Hill 2, thick fog makes the town feel closed in and uneasy. Many games of that time also used fog to hide how little of the world the console could draw at once.

Glow

In Shadow of the Colossus, bright light spills into the areas around it. This effect is called bloom, and it makes the whole land seem to glow.

Color filters

Metal Gear Solid 3: Snake Eater puts a green, sepia-like tint over the whole picture, which gives its jungle a warm, faded look.

CRT TVs

PS2 games were made for CRT TVs. Their soft picture and thin dark lines, called scanlines, hid jagged edges, so games looked smoother on a TV than they do in a sharp screenshot.

Read the transcript

Your screen uses the RGB color model. It makes every color by mixing just three: red, green and blue. Each pixel has a tiny light for each one, and changing how bright they are gives you a different color. Turn all three all the way up and you get white. Turn them all off and you get black.

Your computer stores the brightness of each light as a number from 0 to 255. Each number takes 8 bits, so a pixel uses 24 bits in total. This is called 24-bit color, and it gives you about 16.7 million possible colors.

You’ve probably seen colors written as a code like #D6A860. That’s a hex code, and it’s just those same three numbers written in a shorter way. The first two characters are the red, the middle two are the green, and the last two are the blue. So D6A860 means red 214, green 168 and blue 96, which is a light tan.

When a camera turns a real scene into a digital image, it does two things. First, it splits the scene into a grid of tiny squares called pixels. That step is sampling, and more pixels means more detail. Then comes quantization: each pixel’s color gets rounded to the closest option from a fixed set. So sampling decides how much detail the image has, and quantization decides how many colors it can use.

Color quantization uses that same idea to cut an image down to just a few colors, while keeping it close to the original. Imagine drawing a sunset with only an 8-pack of crayons. You can’t match every shade in the sky, so for each spot you pick the closest crayon you have. The computer does the same thing with every pixel.

Those few colors make up the palette. Instead of storing a full color for every pixel, the image keeps the palette once and gives each pixel a number that points to one of its colors. It works like a paint-by-numbers picture. An image saved this way is called an indexed image, and the palette acts as its lookup table. An 8-bit indexed image can have up to 256 colors, and it takes up much less space than a 24-bit one.

The downside is that smooth areas can lose their smoothness. A sky that slowly fades from light blue to dark blue needs lots of in-between shades. If the palette doesn’t have enough of them, you see stripes of color instead of a smooth fade. Those stripes are called color banding.

Dithering makes an image with only a few colors look smoother. Its main job is to hide banding. To do that, it mixes the colors the image has in a fine pattern of dots. Up close you can see the dots, but from a normal distance your eye blends them into the shades in between. So the sky looks smooth again, even though no new colors were added.

Many PS2 games used this trick because they drew their graphics with fewer colors to save memory. They used a small checkered pattern. Old TVs had a soft picture that blurred the pattern away, so most players never noticed it.

Media file details

The three media files on this site. For each one, the table shows how much smaller it got, how much color or sound detail it keeps, and how it was compressed.

Asset Name & File Format Original Size → Compressed Color Depth / Quantization Compression Algorithm
Sample photo 1 (WebP) 412 KB JPEG → 20 KB WebP. The photo was also resized from 2160 × 3840 to 563 × 1000 pixels. 24-bit color, with 8 bits each for red, green and blue. Lossy WebP at quality 80. It splits the image into small blocks and drops detail your eye is unlikely to notice. Some quality is lost to make the file smaller.
Sample photo 2 (PNG) 3.6 MB JPEG → 366 KB PNG. The photo was also resized from 4928 × 3264 to 1000 × 662 pixels. 8-bit indexed color. The colors were cut down to a palette of 256, with dithering to hide the banding. Lossless PNG, using DEFLATE (LZ77 and Huffman coding). It stores repeated patterns as shorter codes and loses nothing. The only loss happened earlier, when the colors were cut down to 256.
Voice-over (MP3) 16.0 MB WAV → 2.7 MB MP3. Both are 3 minutes 2 seconds long. 16-bit audio at 44.1 kHz, mono. The recording measures the sound 44,100 times per second and rounds each measurement to one of 65,536 levels. Lossy MP3 with a variable bitrate that averages about 119 kbps. It removes sounds people can barely hear, then shrinks the rest with Huffman coding.

1 KB is 1,000 bytes. Part of each photo’s saving comes from the smaller pixel size, not only from compression.
